Spot the Phish

A practical mini-training to help you recognize phishing emails, fake websites, and social-engineering tactics before they put an account or organization at risk.

1. What is phishing?

Phishing is a social-engineering attack in which a message or website impersonates a trusted person or organization to trick someone into revealing information, opening a harmful file, sending money, or approving an account action.

  • Email phishing: a deceptive message sent to many people.
  • Spear phishing: a targeted message tailored to a particular person or team.
  • Smishing / vishing: phishing through SMS or voice calls.
  • Business email compromise: impersonation of a colleague, executive, or supplier to manipulate a payment or sensitive request.

2. Recognize the warning signs

  • Unexpected urgency: “Your account will be closed in 10 minutes.”
  • Sender mismatch: the display name looks familiar, but the actual email domain is different.
  • Suspicious links: a link's displayed text does not match its real destination, or the domain has misspellings or extra words.
  • Unexpected attachments: especially files you were not expecting or files asking you to enable macros.
  • Requests for secrets: passwords, one-time codes, recovery codes, or payment details.
  • Unusual payment or account changes: verify through a separate, known channel.

HTTPS and a padlock only indicate an encrypted connection; they do not prove that a website is legitimate.

3. Example: suspicious email

From: “IT Helpdesk” <helpdesk@company-account-security.example>

Subject: URGENT: mailbox will be disabled today

Your mailbox is full. Sign in immediately using the link below to prevent account closure. Do not contact IT because this automated process must be completed now.

Review mailbox now

Training example only. The example domain is reserved for documentation and is not a real login service.

What should make you suspicious?

  • The sender domain may not match the organization's real domain.
  • The message pressures the recipient to act immediately.
  • It discourages independent verification.
  • It asks the recipient to sign in through a message link.

Safer action: do not click the link. Visit the organization's known website directly or contact the helpdesk using a trusted contact method. Report the message using your organization's approved process.

4. Social engineering tactics

  • Authority: pretending to be a manager or IT administrator.
  • Fear: threatening account suspension or disciplinary action.
  • Scarcity and urgency: claiming an offer or deadline will expire immediately.
  • Familiarity: impersonating a coworker, friend, or supplier.
  • Curiosity: using a surprising document or sensational attachment.

5. Best practices

  • Inspect the full sender address and the actual link destination before interacting.
  • Never share passwords, one-time codes, or recovery codes through email or chat.
  • Use a password manager and unique passwords; enable MFA or passkeys where available.
  • Verify payment changes and sensitive requests through a separate trusted channel.
  • Keep your browser, operating system, and security software updated.
  • Report suspicious messages promptly. If you already clicked or entered credentials, contact IT/security immediately and change affected credentials from a trusted device.
